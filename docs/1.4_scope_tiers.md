# SurgiFlow AI — Project Scope (MVP, Target, and Stretch Goals)

**Kanban Task:** 1.4 — Define MVP, Target, and Stretch Scope

## 1. Overview

For SurgiFlow AI, we want to build a simulation that helps us understand how hospitals can manage their operating rooms and respond when emergency patients arrive unexpectedly.

The challenge is that hospitals have limited resources. Some operating rooms may already be occupied, certain hospitals may not have the specialists needed for a particular surgery, and procedures can take longer than expected. When an emergency occurs, the system needs to decide which hospital should receive the patient and how that hospital can adjust its schedule without causing unnecessary delays.

Since this is a semester project, we need to be realistic about what we can build and evaluate properly. We have therefore divided the project into three stages:

- **MVP (Minimum Viable Product):** A basic but fully working simulation that demonstrates the main idea behind SurgiFlow AI.
- **Target:** The complete version we aim to deliver for CS5100, including optimization algorithms, reinforcement learning, and experimental comparisons.
- **Stretch:** Additional features we would like to explore if we finish the main requirements early.

This approach allows us to focus on getting the essential system working before introducing more complicated algorithms and features.

## 2. Our Starting Simulation Environment

To keep the project manageable, we will begin with a small, fictional hospital network consisting of three hospitals.

| Hospital              | Operating Rooms | ICU Beds | Available Specialties    |
| --------------------- | --------------: | -------: | ------------------------ |
| Central Trauma Center |               4 |        8 | Trauma, Cardiac, General |
| Riverside General     |               3 |        4 | Cardiac, General         |
| Northside Community   |               2 |        2 | General                  |

We will also simulate patients arriving from four different areas: Downtown, East, North, and Suburbs.

Each area will have a predefined travel time to each hospital. For now, we will use these fixed travel times instead of trying to model real traffic or ambulance routes.

Each simulation will represent one day (1,440 minutes). The normal operating-room workday will be 600 minutes, and we will initially aim for approximately 85% elective surgery utilization.

We will simulate around 12 emergency arrivals per day, with three urgency levels and corresponding maximum waiting times of 60, 240, and 1,440 minutes.

These values give us a consistent starting point for testing different scheduling and hospital-allocation strategies. The data and hospitals are fictional, so the simulation is intended for research and learning rather than actual hospital use.

## 3. MVP — What We Need to Build First

Our first priority is to develop a working simulation that can handle scheduled surgeries and unexpected emergency patients.

At this stage, we are not trying to build the smartest scheduling system. We want to make sure the simulation behaves correctly and that we can measure what happens when different decisions are made.

### What the MVP will include

**1. A working hospital simulation**

We will build a discrete-event simulator that keeps track of what happens throughout the day. This includes scheduled surgeries, emergency arrivals, the start and completion of procedures, and changes in operating-room availability.

We will also use fixed random seeds so that we can reproduce the same scenarios when testing our algorithms.

**2. Hospital resources and restrictions**

Each hospital will have its own operating rooms, ICU capacity, and available surgical specialties.

For example, Northside Community cannot receive a patient who needs cardiac surgery because that specialty is not available there.

The simulator must respect these restrictions when making decisions.

**3. Simple scheduling and hospital-selection methods**

Before introducing AI, we will implement straightforward approaches that give us something to compare our more advanced algorithms against.

For surgical scheduling, we will begin with:

- First Come, First Served (FCFS), where patients are handled in arrival order.
- Urgency-first scheduling, where more urgent cases receive higher priority.

For hospital selection, we will compare:

- Choosing the nearest hospital that can treat the patient.
- Choosing a suitable hospital while also considering available capacity and expected delays.

These methods will serve as our baseline approaches.

**4. Handling situations where resources are unavailable**

The simulator should be able to recognize situations where a patient cannot immediately be assigned to a suitable hospital or operating room.

Instead of silently making an invalid assignment, the system will record what happened, including delays, missed waiting-time limits, or situations where no feasible assignment is available.

**5. Measuring performance**

We will collect information that helps us understand how well each approach performs.

This includes how long emergency patients wait before surgery, how often urgency limits are exceeded, delays to planned surgeries, operating-room utilization and overtime, cancellations or disruptions, and how the workload is distributed across hospitals.

**6. Testing and basic visualization**

We will write automated tests to check that the simulator follows its rules. For example, two surgeries should not occupy the same operating room at the same time.

We will also include a simple event log or timeline to help us understand what happened during each simulation.

### When we will consider the MVP complete

The MVP will be complete when we can run a full-day simulation from beginning to end, assign patients using our baseline methods, track the results, and confirm that the system respects its main constraints.

At that point, we should have a reliable foundation for testing more advanced approaches.

## 4. Target — What We Aim to Deliver for CS5100

Once the MVP is working properly, we will expand SurgiFlow AI to better represent the uncertainty and complexity of hospital scheduling.

This is where our optimization algorithms and reinforcement-learning methods become important.

### What the target version will include

**1. More realistic disruptions**

We will introduce variations in surgery duration, procedures running longer than expected, cancellations, and multiple emergencies arriving around the same time.

This will allow us to test how well our scheduling methods respond when the original plan no longer works.

**2. Optimization using Simulated Annealing and Genetic Algorithms**

We will implement Simulated Annealing (SA) and Genetic Algorithms (GA) to explore alternative surgical schedules.

Rather than simply following the original schedule, these methods will search for arrangements that may reduce delays, overtime, and disruptions.

We will also investigate when it makes sense to revise an existing schedule after an unexpected event.

**3. Reinforcement learning using Q-learning and SARSA**

We will build a manageable reinforcement-learning problem where an agent learns from repeated interactions with our simulation.

For example, the agent could learn which eligible hospital to choose for an emergency patient based on travel time, available resources, and expected waiting time.

We will implement Q-learning and SARSA from scratch and define the states, actions, and rewards clearly.

Because this is a semester project, we will keep the state and action spaces small enough for tabular reinforcement learning rather than attempting a large deep-learning system.

**4. Comparing the different approaches**

One of our most important goals is to understand whether the more advanced methods actually improve performance.

We will compare the baseline methods, SA, GA, Q-learning, and SARSA using consistent simulation scenarios and random seeds.

We will also make sure the comparisons are meaningful. For example, if SA and GA are optimizing surgical schedules while Q-learning is selecting hospitals, we will evaluate their respective decisions rather than treating them as identical algorithms.

We will test the methods on scenarios that were not used during development or training and report variations in performance across multiple runs.

**5. Visualization and final analysis**

We will create operating-room schedule visualizations, including before-and-after Gantt charts, to show how schedules change when emergencies occur.

We will also analyze cases where an algorithm performs poorly and explain why.

Finally, we will document our implementation, experimental results, limitations, and conclusions in our project report and presentation.

### When we will consider the target complete

The target will be complete when SA, GA, Q-learning, and SARSA have been implemented for clearly defined problems, tested using repeatable scenarios, and evaluated against appropriate baselines.

We should be able to explain not only which approach performs better in our experiments, but also the conditions under which it performs well or struggles.

## 5. Stretch Goals — What We Can Explore If Time Allows

These features would make SurgiFlow AI more detailed, but they are not necessary for the core project to succeed.

**1. More detailed ICU and recovery-bed management**

We could simulate what happens after a patient leaves the operating room, including recovery-bed availability, ICU occupancy, and patient discharge.

This would help us investigate situations where a hospital has an available operating room but lacks the resources needed to care for the patient afterward.

**2. Larger hospital networks and emergency surges**

We could expand beyond the original three hospitals and test scenarios involving significantly higher emergency demand.

We could also introduce more detailed geographic information, travel routes, and animated ambulance movement.

**3. A more interactive dashboard**

We could develop a polished nine-panel dashboard that allows users to explore the simulation, view hospital resources, examine schedules, and compare algorithm performance.

If time permits, we could also run more extensive experiments to see how sensitive the algorithms are to different parameter settings.

These improvements would be valuable, but we should only begin them after the main simulation and experimental comparisons are working reliably.

---

## 6. Our Development Priorities

The most important thing is to build SurgiFlow AI in the right order.

We should first make sure that the simulator works correctly and produces reliable results. Once that foundation is in place, we can introduce optimization methods and reinforcement learning.

After implementing the algorithms, we need to spend enough time testing them, comparing their performance, and understanding their limitations.

If we try to build every feature at once, we risk ending up with a complicated system that is difficult to debug or evaluate.

Our priority is therefore to deliver a smaller, well-tested project with meaningful results rather than a larger system with features we cannot properly explain or validate.

## 7. Final Scope Statement

By the end of CS5100, our goal is to have a working multi-hospital simulation that demonstrates how emergency arrivals affect hospital allocation and surgical scheduling.

We want to compare simple decision-making rules with optimization and reinforcement-learning methods, examine the trade-offs between patient delays and hospital resource usage, and understand how these approaches respond to unexpected events.

If we accomplish this with a reliable simulator, clear experiments, and well-explained results, we will have achieved the main purpose of SurgiFlow AI.
